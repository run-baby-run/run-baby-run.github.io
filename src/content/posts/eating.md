Sports nutrition is an industry, and industries complicate things. For the runs
most of us do, it is simpler than it looks.

*Genuinely worth saying: this is general information, not advice for you
specifically. If you have a medical condition, diabetes especially, talk to
someone qualified before changing how you eat around exercise.*

## Before

**Under an hour:** nothing required. Run fasted if that suits you, eat if it
doesn't. It makes very little difference at this length.

**Over an hour:** something with carbohydrate one to three hours before. Toast
and honey, a banana, oats, rice. Low in fat and fibre — both slow digestion and
both make themselves known at kilometre eight.

**The Tuesday problem.** For a 22:00 run, your last proper meal was probably
lunch. Something small around eight o'clock — a banana, a slice of bread with
jam — makes the last few kilometres considerably better.

## During

**Under 60–75 minutes:** water if you want it. Nothing else.

**Over 90 minutes:** you need carbohydrate, because your stored glycogen runs
low. Roughly **30–60 g per hour**, starting before you need it rather than after.
Gels, dried fruit, sweets, sports drink — whatever your stomach tolerates.

Practise it on training runs. Race day is the worst possible time to discover
that a particular gel disagrees with you.

## After

Within an hour or two, eat a normal meal with **carbohydrate and protein** in it.
Around 20–25 g of protein is the usual figure for repair. You do not need a
shake; eggs, yoghurt, beans, meat, lentils all do the job.

There is no magic thirty-minute window. It is more forgiving than the marketing
suggests — but after a long run, sooner is better than later.

## Water, and Yerevan in August

Drink to thirst most of the time; it is a better guide than a schedule. In real
heat you also lose salt, so on long hot runs something with sodium in it beats
plain water.

And this city is covered in [pulpulaks](/diary#2026-09-06). Use them. That is
what they are for, and the dog agrees.
