Here is the thing nobody tells you when you start: most of your running should
feel slower than you think it ought to. Not a bit slower. A lot slower.

The reason is that easy running and hard running do different jobs. Easy running
builds the parts of you that take months to change — the capillaries feeding
your muscles, the mitochondria inside them, the tendons, the bones, the sheer
habit of being a person who runs. Hard running sharpens what you already have.
You cannot sharpen what you have not built.

## How easy is easy

The talk test settles it. If you can speak a full sentence without gasping, you
are in the right place. If you can only manage three words, you are running a
tempo session and calling it an easy run.

Most people find this humbling. You will be slower than you expect, and on the
hills around Yerevan you may end up walking a little. Walk. Nobody is watching,
and the run still counts — [we settled that in March](/diary#2026-03-28).

## The split

A rough rule that has held up well: about **four fifths of your running easy,
one fifth hard**. If you run four times a week, that is three easy runs and one
where you actually work.

The trap is the middle. A run that is too hard to recover from but too easy to
improve anything is the least useful run you can do, and it is the one most
beginners do most often. It leaves you permanently a bit tired and never quite
fitter.

## What it buys you

Run easy on the easy days and the hard days get genuinely hard, which is the
point. You also stop collecting the small injuries that come from never being
fresh.

If you want a number to run to rather than a feeling,
[heart rate is a better guide than pace](/blog/heart-rate-not-pace) — especially
here, where nothing is flat.
