The instinct, when you want to go faster, is to reach — longer steps, foot
further forward. It is the wrong instinct, and it has a name.

## Overstriding

If your foot lands well ahead of your hips with the leg straight, three things
happen at once, none of them good.

- You **brake**. The ground pushes back against your forward motion every step.
- The impact goes **up a straight leg** into your knee and hip, rather than being
  absorbed by a bending one.
- You have to **work to get going again** after each landing.

You can feel it: a heavy, slapping sort of run that leaves your shins and knees
sore.

## What to aim for instead

Your foot wants to land more or less **under your centre of mass**, with the knee
softly bent, and your shin close to vertical. Whether you land on your heel or
your midfoot matters much less than where that foot is relative to your hips.

## How to fix it without thinking about your feet

Trying to consciously shorten your steps while running is miserable and rarely
sticks. The reliable fix is indirect: **raise your [cadence](/blog/cadence) by
about five percent and your stride shortens on its own.** There is nowhere else
for it to go.

A short hill also does it for free. Nobody overstrides uphill — the ground
arrives too early. Two or three minutes up any of the streets off Saryan and your
form sorts itself out.

## But stride length does grow

As you get fitter and stronger your stride lengthens by itself, from the back:
you push further behind you rather than reaching further ahead. That is the
version you want, and it arrives on its own if you keep running and do a bit of
[strength work](/blog/strength).
